About

A builder's path from quantum circuits to production machine learning.

I am Pujan Pandey, an AI/ML engineer in Butwal, Nepal. I study computer science, build systems that can be measured, and keep the parts that fail visible.

Butwal, Nepal · NPT (UTC+05:45)pujanpandey012@gmail.com

The short version

I like the part of engineering where the number has to mean something.

My work sits on the engineering side of machine learning. I build pipelines that ingest continuously, models that are evaluated against a stated budget, and interfaces that tell the truth about how fresh a number is. The interesting problems are rarely in the model definition — they are in the data contract, the split, the restart, the alert that nobody trusts.

Before that, quantum computing was my entry point. Implementing Shor's algorithm and Grover's search in Qiskit taught me to reason about state, measurement and probability in a way that transferred directly to thinking about uncertainty in ML systems. It also set the habit: build the thing, then explain precisely why it works and where it stops working.

I am early in my career and deliberate about it. Rather than listing frameworks, I would rather point at the network detection platform that tails real Zeek logs, the GPT-2 clone with a tokenizer written from scratch, and the medical-imaging pipeline where a model only reaches production after passing a gate.

The journey

Four turns that shaped how I build.

The start01

Quantum computing first

Shor's algorithm was the thing that made computation feel strange and worth chasing. I implemented it in Qiskit, then Grover's search, phase estimation, teleportation and superdense coding — not to collect notebooks, but to understand why each step is necessary.

The shift02

From circuits to models

Quantum experiments kept hitting the same wall: the interesting failure was never the maths, it was the data and the evaluation. That pushed me toward applied machine learning — stratified splits, leakage, threshold selection, and metrics that survive a different dataset.

The discipline03

MLOps as the real problem

A model in a notebook is a hypothesis. A model with a pipeline, a registry, a quality gate and a rollback path is a decision. Building the NDR platform and the medical-imaging pipeline taught me where production actually breaks: contracts, drift, restarts and silent failures.

Now04

Systems, not demos

Today I build detection platforms, deep-learning implementations from scratch and agentic systems — each with tests, CI and a limitations section. I would rather publish a 0.00 recall result than pretend a model generalises.

What I actually do

Three kinds of work, all grounded in repositories.

Machine learning engineering

Feature contracts shared between training and inference, threshold selection against a false-positive budget, and evaluation reported with the split it came from.

  • HistGradientBoosting across 298k deduplicated flows
  • ResNet18 transfer learning for chest X-ray triage
  • Cross-dataset probes published, including the failures

MLOps and platform work

Pipelines that someone else can rerun: DVC stages, MLflow runs, model registry promotion, retraining and recovery automation.

  • DVC pipelines with tracked stages and artifacts
  • Quality gates that compare a candidate against a baseline
  • Prometheus and Grafana monitoring for drift and latency

Detection engineering

Turning raw telemetry into alerts an analyst can act on, with the model and the rules kept deliberately separate so each signal stays explainable.

  • Incremental Zeek log ingestion with offset persistence
  • Ten behavioural rule detectors alongside the classifier
  • Alert de-duplication with evidence attached per event

Engineering philosophy

Four rules I do not break for a nicer demo.

These are the habits that came out of building systems meant to run unattended — and out of publishing the numbers that did not flatter me.

  1. 01

    Measure before claiming

    Latency percentiles, throughput and false-positive rates are in the README because they were measured, not estimated.

  2. 02

    Publish the failure modes

    The NDR platform documents a cross-dataset probe with zero recall. Hiding that would make every other number meaningless.

  3. 03

    Reproducibility is a feature

    Pinned dependencies, seeded splits, DVC-tracked artifacts and tests that fail when a contract drifts.

  4. 04

    Keep the interface honest

    Replay data is tagged, an idle sensor says it is idle, and no dashboard fabricates activity to look busy.

Where I am going

The next three problems.

I am looking for a team where machine learning is treated as infrastructure: monitored, versioned and owned by someone.

  • Production ML at a real scale

    Models with SLAs, drift alarms and on-call ownership — the environment where MLOps decisions stop being theoretical.

  • The ML and security intersection

    Detection systems that are honest about adversarial traffic, and ML systems that are themselves defensible.

  • Quantum algorithms with practical intent

    Continuing Qiskit work with an eye on where quantum methods complement classical ML rather than replace it.
